# Moonloom Premise Workshop

Use this reference when the author does not yet have a settled LunaTalk role-card
premise. They may have only a mood, trope, aesthetic, relationship fantasy,
genre cluster, or goal like "make it popular" or "open the idea up."

Premise workshop happens before card blueprinting. It should make the author feel
more choices are possible while still converging toward one playable direction.

## Core rule

Turn taste signals into contrasted playable directions:

```text
taste signal -> contrast axes -> 3 directions -> pressure test -> handoff packet
```

Do not start by writing fields. Do not ask a long interview. If the author is
stuck, propose defaults and make the tradeoffs visible.

Run the audience-legibility gate before recommending any direction. A premise can
be strange, poetic, or stylized, but a normal player should still understand the
card's basic promise in one glance.

```text
recognizable contract -> human-scale first scene -> concrete player role ->
visible pressure -> novelty overlay
```

The recognizable contract is the shelf the player already understands: school
admission, survival, household visit, workplace duty, club activity, academy
training, investigation, travel, competition, shop/inn routine, military
mission, tabletop adventure, simulator, generator, or companion relationship.
Put the unusual idea on top of that contract instead of replacing the contract
with an abstract institution, metaphor, or mood.

Use the five-second test:

- Where am I?
- Who is the role in ordinary terms?
- Who am I in ordinary terms?
- What just happened or is about to happen?
- What can I do in the first reply?

If any answer needs a metaphor, a lore glossary, or "you will understand later",
the direction is not ready. Repair it before blueprinting.

## When to use

Use premise workshop when:

- there is no named role, player position, first scene, or primary card contract
- the author gives a vibe such as cozy, eerie, intense, cute, lonely, forbidden,
  healing, dangerous, funny, surreal, or "top-tier"
- the author asks to brainstorm, open up an idea, explore directions, compare
  options, or choose what kind of card to make
- several genres are possible but none has a clear playable contract yet
- the author is not ready for final fields, MCP calls, render review, or
  simulation

Route away when:

- a concrete premise already exists and the author asks for field-ready output:
  use `lunatalk-card-author`
- the primary card type is the blocker: use `lunatalk-archetype-director`
- one weak layer is explicit: use the narrow skill for character core,
  relationship, world, agency, opening, longplay, token, boundary, scenario,
  daily-life, play engine, ensemble, or presentation
- the author asks whether a draft is good enough: use
  `lunatalk-quality-auditor`

## High-leverage questions

Ask at most two questions before proposing directions. If the author did not ask
to answer questions first, prefer assumptions plus options.

Useful questions:

- Which feeling should survive every route: comfort, danger, intimacy, wonder,
  humor, grief, power, mystery, care, rivalry, or competence?
- How much should the player define themselves: blank-slate, lightly framed, or
  specific role?
- Should romance be central, optional, absent, or a risky route?
- Should the strange element be confirmed, ambiguous, or mundane in disguise?
- Should the card feel like a companion, an incident, a routine, a system, a
  world, or a game?
- What is off-limits in tone or intensity?

## Taste axes

Choose 3-5 axes that matter for this author. The axes should guide direction
design, not become labels.

Common axes:

- warmth vs threat
- everyday vs mythic
- relationship-first vs incident-first
- blank-slate player vs specific player role
- comfort loop vs consequence loop
- slow-burn vs immediate pressure
- ambiguity vs explicit world rule
- small setting vs wide world
- quiet routine vs dramatic event
- playful system vs emotional scene
- low intensity vs boundary-sensitive intensity

## Direction grammar

Each direction should differ by the play contract, not only by aesthetics.

```text
Direction:
- title:
- audience-legible pitch:
- primary contract:
- player role:
- role seed:
- first scene:
- core loop:
- what changes:
- involvement ladder:
- why it fits the author's taste:
- tradeoff / risk:
- best next Moonloom skill:
```

Three strong directions usually cover:

1. the safest playable option
2. the boldest option
3. the most unusual but still controllable option

Do not give ten directions. Too many choices delay authorship.

## Involvement ladder

When the author wants player freedom, define an involvement ladder:

```text
Involvement ladder:
- distant route:
- practical route:
- curiosity / investigation route:
- intimacy / trust route:
- conflict / refusal route:
```

Every route should continue play. Refusal, distance, or low involvement should
shift the loop, not end the card.

## Pressure test

Before recommending a direction, test it quickly:

- Can a player describe the premise to a friend without explaining private lore?
- Does the direction name a recognizable card shelf before its novelty layer?
- Can the first user reply be written in under 10 seconds?
- Does the player have leverage beyond admiring, comforting, or obeying?
- What changes after the first reply?
- Which field would carry durable rules later?
- What is the biggest failure mode: generic trope, lore dump, forced intimacy,
  passive role, fake choices, token bloat, or unclear rating?
- Which narrow Moonloom skill should run next?

Recommend the direction that has the clearest first reply and best second-turn
engine, not the one with the largest lore or prettiest mood.

Reject the "abstract prestige" failure mode: a beautifully named place, object,
or ritual that does not tell the player what kind of story this is. Replace it
with a human-scale situation first, such as arriving for a job, starting school,
opening a locked room, choosing a route, surviving an attack, negotiating with a
roommate, or meeting a guide at a concrete location.

## Common repairs

| Failure | Repair |
|---|---|
| Vibe only | convert mood into player role, pressure, and first scene |
| "Popular card" request | define public craft goals; do not attach unsupported performance claims |
| Too many genres | pick one primary contract and keep the rest as overlays |
| Romance becomes assumed | define romance as central, optional, absent, or gated |
| Mystery has no leverage | give the player clue, access, suspicion, or choice |
| Cozy becomes no action | add tiny disruption, shared object, or small cost |
| Eerie becomes shock | tie strangeness to motive, route, or consequence |
| Light fantasy becomes lore dump | keep one rule and show it through action |
| Player freedom becomes blankness | define player controls and route options |
| Poetic but confusing premise | attach it to a recognizable shelf, ordinary player role, concrete place/time, and first action |
| Abstract institution or object dominates | translate it into a job, school, mission, survival problem, relationship obligation, investigation, or simulator contract |
| First scene starts with atmosphere only | begin with a person doing something to the player, a form to fill, a door to enter, a threat to answer, or a route to choose |

## Public-safety posture

Keep the output original and public-safe. Do not attach unsupported origin or
performance claims. If the author asks for "top card" quality, translate that
into craft goals: clear promise, strong player agency, memorable role behavior,
consequential second turn, token discipline, and testable handoff.
